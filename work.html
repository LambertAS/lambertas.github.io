Employee pulse survey analytics pipeline

The annual employee engagement survey runs across the whole organisation. It reaches over 12,000 employees, 11 directorates, and 88 reporting units. This was a pulse measurement ahead of it, roughly 9,000 responses to process, checking whether managers had acted on the previous year's results. The team had assembled that reporting by hand each year, which limited how much they could report and how fast.

I built the pipeline that replaced it. Raw export goes in, mapped to org units, stratified sampling applied, and a formatted PowerPoint report comes out for each unit.

The org-unit mapping caused the most trouble. Reporting structures do not match the shape of the data export, and getting responses attributed to the right unit without breaking respondent anonymity took more iteration than the analysis did.

I tested simple random sampling first, against the previous year's data. It produced unstable favorability on small units, where a handful of respondents meant one or two people could move the percentage by several points. Stratified sampling, tried in response, held up: respondents split into three strata by mean score, slots allocated proportionally with largest-remainder rounding, and a fixed seed for reproducibility.

Mean-fill imputation could have filled the gaps. I rejected it because it manufactures answers no respondent gave. A script instead verified that every output row existed unmodified in the input, so nothing downstream stood on data that hadn't come from someone answering.

The open-text responses were in Indonesian, themed with whole-word regular expressions tolerant of Indonesian prefixes and suffixes rather than plain substring matching, which over-counted. Short tokens kept matching inside longer, unrelated words. The corrected pass reached 81.5% thematic coverage, and a manual review on top of it still surfaced two themes the automated pass had missed.

Data quality remediation

211 rows in the previous year's survey data didn't line up. Answer columns had come apart from the demographic columns next to them. Someone had sorted the demographic columns in a spreadsheet without extending the selection to the answer columns, so the two halves of every row separated. I wrote a prevention procedure afterwards, and the mistake set the pattern for most of this work. Data looks fine on inspection and only breaks once it's aggregated or joined.

Four years of service ticket history, 2022 to 2026, needed the same kind of attention before any of it could be trusted. Cleaning covered ghost-column detection and removal, identifying which columns were approval columns, and forward-filling across thousands of empty cells where a blank meant the row was still in the same context as the one above it, not that data was missing.

A later system upload produced hundreds of duplicated transaction records in the order data. I filtered the query, wrote an anomaly report, and recommended a review of the data input flow that had let the duplicates through in the first place.

A response-rate drop in one part of the organisation looked at first like a weighting error. The raw data showed something different. Respondents had selected the wrong unit themselves. That changed the fix from a formula correction to form design and input validation.

Store internship scoring tracker and reminders

A one-year store internship programme scores each intern every quarter. The Store Leader fills in a Google Form, quarters count from that intern's own start date, and a cross-training stage follows the fourth. The talent team merged the form responses with an HR system export in an Excel workbook built on Power Query, then chased Store Leaders by email and WhatsApp when scores were missing. An intern with a missing score gets no certificate and does not officially graduate, even when the gap is the Store Leader's.

I mapped the process and its problems, then put options to the team. They wanted a small fix inside the Google tools they already used. I moved the workbook to Google Sheets with formulas that recalculate live and extend to new rows on their own, built on ARRAYFORMULA, FILTER, SORT, QUERY and XLOOKUP, with named ranges tied to column headers.

Moving it exposed four faults in the original workbook. Duplicate form submissions were added together instead of keeping the latest. Scores stored as text dropped out of the totals. The per-store summary undercounted because its formulas stopped at the last row that existed when they were written. A locale setting had swapped day and month on more than 700 start dates, and every quarter counts from that date. I fixed all four and added a completion rate for each store.

I then wrote a Google Apps Script reminder that sends each store one email with a table of its interns whose scores are missing, the quarters affected and the due dates. It has a preview, a test send and a send log. It blocks a second send on the same day and can resume when the daily email quota runs out partway. Run against the data at testing time, it produced 154 store emails covering 470 interns who needed follow-up. It runs from a company Google Workspace account with two permissions: spreadsheet access and sending email.

HR service ticket SLA dashboard

The report already existed as an Excel file. It showed one period, it could not be filtered, and updating it meant someone opening the file and editing it again by hand every cycle.

I rebuilt it in Power BI, covering ticket data from 2022 to 2026. I reshaped the source data, then built the visualisations and interactive filters, choosing each chart type to fit what the measure was rather than applying one format across everything.

Visualisation wasn't where the real difficulty was. Resolution targets varied by every combination of ticket category and sub-category. A single formula couldn't capture that, so I designed a separate lookup dimension table against the ticket fact table. The measures look up the threshold.

Cleaning was the hard part, and it came before any of the visual work. Not every ticket in the export was in a finished state. Some were still open and some had not been accepted yet, and those cannot be counted alongside closed tickets without distorting every SLA figure on the page. Getting that state logic right determined whether anything downstream meant what it appeared to mean.

I presented the finished dashboard to my Department Head and the General Managers and explained how it worked and why I built each chart the way I did.

Outside the SLA dashboard, I built a workplace accident dashboard covering 2025 through Q1 2026, using a relational model with an incident fact table against time, department, and injury-type dimensions. I also built an AI-usage survey dashboard for HR, then evaluated Looker as a non-Microsoft alternative, building a working relational model and an experimental dashboard in it.

Performance Management System dashboard prototypes

I prototyped the dashboard for the Performance Management System revamp across three access levels: Associate, People Leader, and the Admin panel. Each role sees a different slice of the system, so each needed its own layout rather than one screen with sections hidden depending on who is looking.

The original design showed identical elements to every role, which cross-divisional review flagged as both confusing for operational staff and exposing administrative information to people who didn't need it. I restructured it into role-separated views. Restricting the deadline countdown to Admin was an access-rights decision on least-privilege grounds, not a visual one. I also removed a redundant overview, standardised headers, and added an eligibility disclaimer.

Beyond the role separation, the visual layer changed too: cleaner charts, KPI cards chosen because they were relevant rather than because the data happened to be available, and a more compact layout so a leader can read their view without scrolling past everything else first.

I also supported my team on the wider functionality revamp of the Performance Management System, with the dashboard as my specific contribution.

Requirements work on HR system upgrades

I wrote two Business Requirement Documents and contributed to a third, all taken into build: one covering a mailing services replacement with a supporting cost-benefit analysis, one for a dashboard change request covering all three access levels, and a contribution to a third covering other feature changes. I wrote each one in Bahasa Indonesia to an internal formatting standard.

I translated what HR described into functional specifications IT could estimate against.

User acceptance testing, performance appraisal module

I ran UAT in two phases. Phase one covered the individual and team goal-setting modules. Phase two covered supervisor-level views, the personal development plan module, and mid-year review. I wrote scenarios and test cases up front, before execution, then ran 15 scenarios and logged 20 defects to an issue log, retesting each to closure before launch. I wrote the end-user manual that shipped with it.

The defects were functional. Buttons that did nothing when clicked. Filters that returned the wrong set of records. Fields with no validation at all, and fields accepting special characters they should have rejected. Several sat in the record operations themselves, where adding, editing or deleting did not behave the way the specification said it would.

Batch transcription of Indonesian voice recordings

I was asked to find a way to transcribe a batch of Indonesian voice recordings. The transcripts were my part; another team handled the analysis. I compared free and paid tools, local and cloud, and wrote a one-page brief for the Department Head. I looked at free options first and treated personal data protection and cross-border data transfer as selection criteria.

I tested on three sample recordings. The real batch turned out to be about 60 recordings of roughly 30 minutes each, about 30 hours of audio, and it arrived on the day of the deadline. I rescoped and built a Google Colab notebook running faster-whisper (large-v3) on a GPU. It reads from Google Drive, filters out silence and skips files it has already finished, so a disconnect loses one file at most. It logs progress and writes one text file per recording, named after the source file. A vocabulary prompt helps with speech that mixes Indonesian and English.
